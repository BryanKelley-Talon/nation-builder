About Nation Builder

Nation Builder is a free history and civics simulation from Flashpoint History. It is a modified version of micropolisJS, Graeme McCutcheon's JavaScript port of Micropolis, the open-source release of a city simulator once sold by Electronic Arts. It is not the original program, and it has no affiliation with Electronic Arts.

The game code is free software under the GNU GPL v3 with additional terms (full license text, what we changed). Scenario and lesson content is a separate work under its own license (content license).

Overview

Welcome to Nation Builder. I'm your advisor — I've seen more towns rise and fall than I can count, and I'm here to help you build one that lasts.

You're about to found a town at a real moment in history — this scenario drops you into a real year, no time machine, no reskin. Before your first road goes down, you'll choose how you govern: how much you weigh individual liberty against collective security. That choice isn't cosmetic. It shapes how your town grows, what your citizens tolerate, and what kind of leader you turn out to be. There's no wrong pole. There's only the one you can defend.

One more thing before you start: name your town. Make one up — not your own name, not anyone real. This town is yours to build, not to sign.

Basic overview

Here's what's on your toolbar, mayor.

  • Residential zones give your citizens somewhere to live. No rooftops, no workforce, no town.
  • Commercial zones are where your citizens shop and work retail. They need residents nearby to survive, and they feed your tax base.
  • Industrial zones are where the real jobs and the real money come from — and the real pollution, if you're not careful where you put them.
  • Police and Fire stations protect what you've built. Leave a neighborhood outside their reach and you'll hear about it.
  • Power plants keep the lights on, literally — nothing runs without power, and every plant has a cost, whether that's money, land, or something else.
  • Stadium, Airport, and Seaport are the big civic wins — expensive, but they're what turns a town into a city people know by name.
  • Parks cost you land and money for nothing you can tax directly. Your citizens will thank you anyway.
  • Road, rail, and power lines are how everything else connects. A zone that isn't connected doesn't grow.

Lay these down with the terrain in mind. Rivers, hills, and forests aren't just scenery — they'll make some of these choices for you.

Best practices

A few things I've learned the hard way, so you don't have to.

Watch your budget before it watches you. Taxes fund everything — police, fire, roads — but tax too hard and growth slows down. Check your budget window before it becomes an emergency, not after.

Use the evaluation tool. It tells you, in your citizens' own words, what's actually bothering them — not enough jobs, too much crime, whatever it is. Read it like a town hall, not a report card.

Auto-budget, auto-bulldoze, and auto-disasters are in your settings, and each one trades control for convenience. Auto-budget balances your books for you but takes the wheel away from your own tax decisions. Auto-bulldoze clears obstacles automatically when you zone — faster, less deliberate. Auto-disasters lets the game throw the occasional disaster at you — leave it off if you'd rather build in peace, on if you want to be tested.

Your progress saves as you go — a code for picking up where you left off, and a downloadable file if you want your city with you. You won't finish a town in one sitting, and you're not meant to. Every so often, I'll check in with you on how it's going. Pay attention to those check-ins — they're where you'll actually see what your choices added up to.

Build something you'd want to live in.
